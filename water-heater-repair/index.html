Water Heater Repair in Round Rock, TX

Water heater repairs for no hot water, leaks, noises, and pilot faults.

Call (737) 215-4933 Ask a question

No hot water at every faucet and water collecting beneath a heater often get called the same problem, but they lead us to different parts of the unit. From the hallway, a dry utility closet while every shower and sink runs cold points toward the burner, element, thermostat, or power supply; water crossing the closet threshold points toward a tank, valve, fitting, or drain connection. We handle both water heater faults in Round Rock, Brushy Creek, Windemere, and Wells Branch.

A heater that stays cold but holds water can usually wait for a planned repair, although a gas smell, burning odor, or tripped breaker changes that assessment. A steady puddle, a wet platform, or water running from the tank jacket should not wait because water can spread into drywall, flooring, and the framing around the closet. We isolate the water source, test the heater components, and repair the part that caused the loss of hot water or the leak.

No hot water or puddle

“No hot water” may mean the water stays cold at every fixture, or it may mean a shower turns cold after only a short run. A popping or rumbling sound from the tank can follow sediment buildup around the burner or lower element. A puddle under the heater, rust-colored water at a hot tap, or moisture at the temperature-and-pressure relief valve points us toward a different set of parts.

Gas water heaters can also stop heating after a pilot goes out, a thermocouple fails, or the burner does not receive gas. Electric models may lose one heating element and still produce a small amount of warm water before going cold. These calls can sound alike from the kitchen or bathroom, but the tank, valve, burner, element, and control each require separate work and carry different parts requirements.

Water heater inspection

We start the callout by checking the model plate, tank capacity, fuel type, shutoff valves, drain connection, and the floor around the heater. On an electric unit, we test the disconnect, thermostat, and elements at the access panels; on a gas unit, we inspect the pilot, igniter, burner, vent pipe, and gas control. We also check water temperature and inspect the relief valve, which opens at about 150 psi or 210°F when it operates correctly.

A straightforward inspection and component test commonly takes 30 to 60 minutes, depending on whether the heater sits in a closet, attic, garage, or cabinet. We can often replace a thermostat, heating element, pilot component, relief valve, or supply connector during the same visit if the correct part is available and the shutoff works. A leaking tank, inaccessible vent connection, replacement heater, or ordered control valve usually calls for a return appointment after we confirm the model and installation requirements.

Shutoff valves and access

The age and condition of the heater matter because a newer tank with a failed element or gas control can make sense to repair, while corrosion around the tank seam changes the scope of the work. A model plate and serial number help identify the correct thermostat, element, burner assembly, anode rod, or control valve. Parts that are discontinued or specific to a tankless unit may need to be ordered rather than fitted from standard stock.

Access also moves the work involved. A heater behind a narrow closet door, above a ceiling opening, or in an attic may require more time around the drain pan, vent pipe, water lines, and flooring. A seized cold-water shutoff can add work before we can safely remove a valve or disconnect the heater, and a replacement installation may require permit or inspection sign-off depending on the location and work involved.

Model plate and closet access

Before calling, check the model number and serial number on the manufacturer plate, usually attached to the side of the tank or behind a front panel on some tankless units. Note whether the heater sits in a garage, utility closet, attic, or cabinet, and whether a puddle is present at the base, relief valve, or pipe above it. If hot water still runs, tell us whether every faucet stays cold or whether the temperature drops only after several minutes.

A photo of the plate, the shutoff valve, and the space in front of the heater helps us identify likely parts and plan access. Keep the area around the tank clear, but do not remove an electrical panel or relight a pilot if you smell gas.
