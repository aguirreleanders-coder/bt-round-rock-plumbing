Toilet Repair in Round Rock, TX

Toilet repair for running tanks, weak flushes, clogs, and leaks in Round Rock.

Call (737) 215-4933 Ask a question

A plunger is the sensible first tool for a slow or blocked toilet. It can clear paper lodged near the bowl trapway and restore a normal flush without removing the tank lid or disconnecting the water line. The plunger usually stops helping when the fault sits in the fill valve, flapper, flush valve, handle, or seal beneath the bowl.

A toilet that needs several flushes, refills without stopping, or leaves water around the base needs more than repeated plunging. A running tank can sometimes wait briefly while the bowl still flushes and the floor stays dry, but water at the base, an overflowing bowl, or a toilet that will not shut off should not wait because it can damage flooring and keep sending water through the fixture. We inspect the tank, bowl, shutoff, and floor connection to find the part causing the failure.

Toilet keeps running or won’t flush

“My toilet keeps running” often points to a flapper that does not seal, a fill valve that will not close, or a refill tube placed incorrectly inside the overflow tube. “My toilet will not flush” can mean the handle has disconnected from the lift chain, the flush valve has failed, or the bowl trapway has a blockage. A weak flush, a tank that fills slowly, or a flush that stops halfway each leaves different clues in the bowl and tank.

Water pooling around the toilet base, a bowl that rocks on the tile, or a toilet that backs up after flushing calls for a separate check of the wax ring, closet bolts, flange, and drain opening. A toilet that makes a hissing sound can have a leaking fill valve even when the bowl looks normal. A fill valve, flapper, clogged trapway, and loose base can appear similar from across the bathroom, but each fault involves different parts and different repair work.

Tank and bowl inspection

We start at the toilet with the tank lid, flush handle, fill valve, flapper, lift chain, refill tube, and overflow tube. A dye test can show a flapper leak in about fifteen minutes, while a pressure gauge at the supply line helps us check the water feeding the tank. We also inspect the bowl water level, test the flush, and identify whether the flush valve uses a two-inch or three-inch opening.

A worn flapper, fill valve, handle, chain, or supply connector can often be replaced during the same inspection when the shutoff valve works and the needed part fits the fixture. We check the angle stop before disconnecting a supply line, since a seized valve changes the work needed at the wall. A cracked porcelain tank, corroded closet flange, damaged floor, or model-specific flush tower may require a matched part or a return visit to remove and reset the bowl with a new wax ring.

Fill valve, shutoff, and access

The age and design of the tank parts affect the repair work. A common flapper or fill valve inside an accessible tank involves different parts than an older flush tower, proprietary canister, or discontinued handle assembly. A model number on the porcelain or tank lid helps identify whether a matching component can be obtained without ordering it.

Access also changes the work around the toilet base and water line. A working angle stop allows us to isolate the fixture before replacing a fill valve or supply connector, while a seized shutoff may need attention before the tank can be serviced safely. Tight tile, damaged flooring, a corroded closet flange, or plumbing changes that require sign-off can add work beyond the tank repair, especially if the bowl must come off the floor.

Tank model and bowl location

Before calling, note which bathroom contains the toilet and whether it is the only fixture in that room. If you can do so safely, photograph the manufacturer name and model stamp inside the rear wall of the tank or on the underside of the tank lid, if one is present. Let us know the approximate age of the toilet, whether the bowl runs after flushing, and whether water has reached the tile or floor.

Describe the route to the bathroom, any narrow doorway, and whether the angle stop beside the toilet turns or appears corroded. A photo of the tank interior can help distinguish a flapper, canister, or flush tower before the inspection.
