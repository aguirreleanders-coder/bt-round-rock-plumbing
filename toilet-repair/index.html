Toilet Repair in Round Rock, TX

Toilet repairs for running tanks, weak flushes, leaks, and loose handles in Round Rock.

Call (737) 215-4933 Ask a question

Most people reach for a plunger first, and that makes sense when paper or waste has slowed the bowl. A few steady plunges can restore a normal flush without taking apart the tank or removing the toilet. The plunger stops helping when the blockage sits beyond the trapway, the water rises in the bowl, or the flush valve inside the tank no longer opens correctly.

We also see homeowners jiggle a loose handle, shorten a slack chain, or press down on a flapper that will not seal. Those small adjustments can stop a running tank for a while, but a worn rubber flapper, cracked overflow tube, or failing fill valve usually brings the sound back. Water that keeps entering the tank can run through the bowl long after the bathroom has gone quiet.

A toilet with a stiff handle or occasional weak flush can often wait for a planned repair visit if the bowl stays at its normal water level. Water spreading from the base, a bowl that threatens to overflow, or a tank that runs continuously should not wait, because the wax ring, flooring, and water bill can all worsen while the fixture keeps leaking.

Toilet keeps running

Customers often tell us the toilet keeps running, the tank will not fill, the handle does nothing, or the bowl will not flush all the way. A hissing tank can point to a fill valve that will not shut off, while water trickling into the bowl often points to a flapper or flush valve seal. A handle that moves freely without flushing can come from a broken lever arm, disconnected chain, or warped flapper.

Water around the toilet base, a rocking bowl, sewer-like odor near the floor, and gurgling after a flush need a different check from a running tank. A loose closet bolt, failed wax ring, blocked trapway, or damaged flange can create similar symptoms from outside the bowl. Each complaint can lead to a different part and a different amount of work, even when the sound or puddle looks much the same before we inspect it.

Toilet callout inspection

We start by flushing the toilet, watching the bowl level, and listening to the fill valve after the flush cycle ends. We remove the tank lid, check the handle, chain, flapper, overflow tube, and water line, then inspect the shutoff valve and supply connection. A standard tank check commonly takes about 15 to 30 minutes, depending on whether the bowl and base also need testing.

We use a dye test to find water slipping past the flapper, measure the tank water line against the overflow tube, and check the floor around the bowl for movement or moisture. A 2-inch or 3-inch flush valve needs the matching seal and flapper style, so we identify the fitting before replacing it. We also check whether the shutoff valve turns cleanly before disconnecting the supply tube.

Flappers, fill valves, handles, chains, and supply tubes can often be replaced during the same visit after we confirm the failed part. A toilet that needs to be pulled for a wax ring, flange repair, damaged subfloor, or seized shutoff may need a return visit, especially if the correct flange part or bowl-specific component has to be ordered. We test the flush, refill, and base after the repair before putting the tank lid back.

Fill valves and shutoffs

The age and condition of the fill valve, flapper, flush valve, and handle move the repair cost because a single worn tank part takes less work than several aging fittings. A corroded tank bolt, cracked overflow tube, or specialty flush assembly can add labor and require a part that matches the toilet model. A toilet with a standard replacement fitting usually stays simpler than a fixture with discontinued internal parts.

Access also matters around the toilet and behind the tank. A shutoff valve that has seized can turn a tank repair into a valve replacement, while a bowl that must be lifted can expose a damaged wax ring, flange, or floor. Parts that must be ordered, changes requiring a local sign-off, and tight bathroom access all add steps beyond a straightforward flapper or handle replacement.

Tank model and bowl location

Before calling, check which bathroom holds the toilet and whether water is running into the bowl right now. A photo of the tank interior, handle, flapper, and water level can help us identify the likely fitting before the visit. Look for the manufacturer name and model marking inside the tank, near the water line, or under the tank lid.

Let us know roughly when the toilet was installed, whether the bowl rocks on the floor, and whether water appears only after flushing. Clear the floor around the base and leave access to the shutoff valve behind the bowl if furniture or storage blocks it.
